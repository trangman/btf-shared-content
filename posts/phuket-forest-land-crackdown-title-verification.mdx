<QuickAnswer>

Inspectors focused on mountainous, deforested plots and will verify whether title documents held by private occupants at those sites were lawfully issued.[^1]

## What Triggered the Expanded Sweep

Deputy director-general Phatpong Samittipat said the sweep followed Internal Security Operations Command intelligence on suspected encroachment at eight sites, covering Thep Krasattri, Choeng Thale, Sri Sunthorn, Rawai, Kamala and Patong.[^1]

Six areas, not one hotspot. Enforcement has moved from last month's single Nui Beach dispute, where roughly 500 structures were removed under a court order, to a province-wide review backed by [AMLO](https://www.amlo.go.th) asset tracing.[^1][^2][^3] One target, a Patong restaurant, is reported linked to an influential figure; officials named no individual.[^1]

## Why a Title Deed Is Not the End of the Story

Some plots are held by private parties with title documents, and officials will verify whether those documents were lawfully issued. A Chanote in hand is evidence of registration, not proof the underlying grant was valid, particularly near a forest-reserve boundary.[^1][^2]

A title survives a paperwork review far more easily than a review of how it was granted. Flat-land Chanote issued decades ago rarely draws scrutiny; hillside and near-boundary plots do, and the same question is being asked, more quietly, on Koh Samui.

## What This Means for Existing At-Risk Property-Owning Companies

An owner holding Phuket land through an at-risk property-owning company faces two exposures on one asset: the corporate structure and the land title. Divestment addresses only the first.

Planned divestment, typically eight to sixteen weeks, removes the [Department of Business Development](https://www.dbd.go.th) risk that shareholding never reflected genuine Thai control. A bad title is untouched by that fix; its remedy runs against the land itself, through the [Land Department](https://www.dol.go.th) and the courts. Fixing the company first is the costlier mistake.
